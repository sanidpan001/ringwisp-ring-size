    3

    The paper strip sizer

    Cut a 10 mm-wide strip of paper, wrap it around the finger and slide a pin through the overlap. Open it flat: the pin-to-end distance in millimetres is your circumference. Paper stretches a little, so measure twice and take the average — and measure in the evening.

    Golden rules before you order

    • ◆ Measure in the evening, when fingers are at their largest.
    • ◆ Measure the exact finger — dominant hands run ~½ size larger.
    • ◆ Wide bands (over 6 mm) need half a size up; slim bands can size down.
    • ◆ Big knuckles? Size to clear the knuckle, not the base.
    • ◆ Hands must be at room temperature — cold shrinks, heat swells.
    • ◆ Between sizes? Comfort beats precision — pick the one that slides over the knuckle with slight resistance.

    The definitive guide

    Ring sizes, explained properly

    Buying a ring should be romantic, not stressful — yet nothing ruins the moment like a beautiful ring that will not slide past the knuckle. The good news is that ring sizing is not mysterious at all. Every sizing system on earth describes exactly the same physical thing: the inside circumference of the ring, measured in millimetres. The only difference is the label each country sticks on that measurement. A ring size converter simply translates those labels, and once you understand the handful of systems in use, you can shop for rings anywhere in the world with total confidence.

    Why one finger has five different sizes

    There are five major ring sizing systems, and a single finger can legitimately be called five different “sizes” at once. The US and Canadian system uses numbers and half-numbers — 6, 6.5, 7 — where each whole size represents about 0.8 mm of inner diameter. The UK, Australian and New Zealand system uses letters: each half US size equals one full UK letter, so the alphabet marches upward from F through Z and beyond. The EU / ISO system (also called the French system) is the most honest of all: the number is the inner circumference in millimetres, so EU 54 means 54 mm around the inside. The Japanese and Chinese system uses whole numbers that run roughly as the inner circumference minus 40. And the Swiss system, born in the Swiss jewellery industry, follows nearly the same convention as the Japanese one: Swiss size equals inner circumference in millimetres minus 40, rounded to the nearest whole number. Because all five describe the same circle, conversion between them is exact — there is no opinion involved, only arithmetic and published charts.

    The ring size chart in mm: your anchor measurement

    Millimetres are the universal language underneath every system, which is why a ring size chart in mm is the most reliable reference you can own. Two numbers matter. The inner diameter is the straight-line distance across the inside of the ring — a US size 7 measures 17.3 mm across, a US 6 measures 16.5 mm, and a US 8 measures 18.1 mm. The inner circumference is the distance around the inside — simply the diameter multiplied by pi — and it is what the EU number reports directly. Whenever systems disagree by a hair (and published charts do differ by fractions of a millimetre between manufacturers), the millimetre measurement is the ground truth to trust. That is why RingWisp always shows diameter and circumference alongside every conversion: the numbers behind the labels are what actually determine fit.

    US to UK ring size: the conversion everyone looks up

    The single most-searched conversion in jewellery is US to UK ring size, usually by shoppers eyeing a ring on an American site while living under the British letter system — or the reverse. The relationship is beautifully simple: each half-step in the US equals one full letter in the UK. US 6 is UK L, US 6.5 is UK M, US 7 is UK N, US 7.5 is UK O, US 8 is UK P, US 8.5 is UK Q, US 9 is UK R, US 9.5 is UK S, US 10 is UK T, and the pattern continues up the alphabet. Larger than Z, the UK system keeps counting with Z+1, Z+2 and so on. One caution: some jewellers stock quarter sizes, and vintage charts occasionally disagree by half a letter, so for anything expensive — an engagement ring, a wedding band — confirm against the millimetre diameter rather than trusting a letter alone.

    Japan to US ring size: decoding the Asian system

    Japan to US ring size conversion trips people up because the Japanese numbers look nothing like the American ones — a Japanese 14 sounds enormous next to a US 7, yet they are the same ring. The Japanese scale starts at an inner diameter of 13 mm for size 1 and climbs by one-third of a millimetre per size. In practice, you rarely need the formula because the mapping is fixed: Japan 12 is US 6, Japan 14 is US 7, Japan 17 is US 8, Japan 19 is US 9, Japan 22 is US 10, and Japan 24 is US 11. The same numbers are used across China, South Korea and much of Southeast Asia, so one lookup covers the whole region. If you remember only one rule of thumb, make it this: subtract 40 from the inner circumference in millimetres and you land very close to both the Japanese and the Swiss size.

    How to measure ring size at home

    You do not need a jeweller visit to get an accurate size — three at-home methods, done carefully, get you within a quarter size of a professional measurement. Method one, the string method: wrap a thin strip of paper or a piece of non-stretchy string snugly (not tightly) around the base of the finger, mark where it overlaps, lay it flat and measure the length in millimetres. That length is your inner circumference — feed it straight into the measurement mode of the converter above. Method two, an existing ring: find a ring that already fits the target finger comfortably, lay it on a ruler and measure the inside diameter edge to edge in millimetres, or compare it against the printable true-scale circles above. Method three, the paper strip sizer: cut a strip about 10 mm wide, wrap it around the finger, push a pin through the overlap, then measure from the pin to the end of the strip. Whichever method you choose, measure twice and average the results, use a ruler with millimetre markings (not a soft tape alone, which can stretch), and write the number down immediately — memory is a poor measuring instrument.

    Finger-specific fitting: the details most guides skip

    Here is where most online guides stop and where real-world fit actually lives. Dominant versus non-dominant hand: the hand you write with does more work, carries slightly more muscle, and typically measures about half a US size larger than the same finger on the other hand. Always measure the exact finger that will wear the ring — a size taken on the left ring finger will feel tight on the right. Wide-band adjustment: a slim 2 mm band and a chunky 8 mm band in the “same” size do not feel the same, because a wider band contacts more skin and resists sliding over the knuckle. The industry rule is simple: for bands wider than about 6 mm, go up half a US size; for bands under 4 mm, size down if you fall between two sizes. Time of day: fingers are living tissue. They are slimmest in the cool morning, swell through the afternoon, and reach their largest in the warm evening — heat, salty food, exercise, alcohol and even a long flight can add a noticeable fraction of a size. The safest single measurement you can take is in the evening, at normal room temperature, on a day you have not just exercised. Two more variables worth knowing: prominent knuckles mean you should size to clear the knuckle rather than hug the base (the ring will sit slightly looser at the base, and that is correct), and cold hands can measure a full half size smaller than the same hands an hour later — never size a ring on winter-cold fingers.

    Half sizes, comfort fit, and resizing

    Between two whole sizes? You are in good company — a large share of adults genuinely fall between standard sizes, which is exactly why half sizes exist and why most jewellers stock them. If your measurement lands between, say, US 7 and 7.5, consider the band: slim band, take the 7; wide band, take the 7.5. “Comfort fit” bands, which are slightly domed on the inside, also slide on more easily than flat interiors and can let you wear a quarter size smaller than usual. As for resizing: plain gold and platinum bands without stones can usually be resized up or down by about one full size by any competent jeweller, but eternity bands (stones all the way around), tension settings, and some titanium or tungsten rings cannot be resized at all. When resizing is impossible, getting the size right the first time is not a luxury — it is the whole game.

    Buying rings internationally without fear

    Cross-border ring shopping is where a converter earns its keep. A “size 54” on a European site, a “size N” on a British site and a “size 7” on an American site are the same ring — but only if you verify. Always convert through the millimetre diameter as a sanity check, because regional manufacturing tolerances mean two charts can disagree by a quarter size. Read the seller's own size guide before ordering (reputable sellers publish the diameter behind each size), check the return and complimentary-resize policy, and for surprise proposals, borrow one of their well-fitting rings and measure its inner diameter in secret — it is far more reliable than guessing from a finger traced on paper. If you are between sizes and the seller offers free resizing, order the slightly larger size: sizing down is almost always easier than sizing up.

    When to get professionally sized

    At-home methods are excellent for fashion rings, gifts and first-pass estimates, but three situations deserve a jeweller's mandrel: an expensive engagement or wedding ring, a wide or unusually shaped band, and any finger with arthritis or a very large knuckle relative to its base. A professional sizing takes two minutes, is usually free, and removes the last shred of doubt.     Questions

    Frequently asked

    How do I convert a US ring size to a UK ring size? +

    Every half US size equals one full UK letter: US 6 = UK L, US 7 = UK N, US 8 = UK P, US 9 = UK R. Type your US size into the converter above for the instant UK equivalent plus EU, Japanese and Swiss sizes.

    What is my ring size in millimetres? +

    In millimetres, ring size means inner diameter: US 7 is 17.3 mm, US 6 is 16.5 mm, US 8 is 18.1 mm. The EU number is the inner circumference instead (EU 54 = 54 mm around). Use measurement mode above — enter your mm and get every system.

    How can I measure my ring size at home without a ring sizer? +

    Wrap paper or non-stretchy string snugly around the finger base, mark the overlap and measure the length in millimetres — that is your inner circumference. Or measure the inner diameter of a ring that already fits. Enter either number above.

    How do I convert a Japanese ring size to a US size? +

    Japan 12 = US 6, Japan 14 = US 7, Japan 17 = US 8, Japan 19 = US 9. As a rule of thumb, Japanese size is roughly the inner circumference in mm minus 40. The converter above looks up exact Japan-to-US matches across the full chart.

    Should I size up for a wide band ring? +

    Yes — bands wider than about 6 mm fit tighter, so go up half a US size (e.g. 7 to 7.5). Slim bands under 4 mm can take the smaller size when you are between sizes. RingWisp's fit tips apply this adjustment to your result automatically.

    Does my ring size change during the day? +

    Yes. Fingers are smallest in the cool morning and largest in the warm evening; heat, salt, exercise and flights all add size. Measure in the evening at room temperature, and remember the dominant hand runs about half a size larger.
